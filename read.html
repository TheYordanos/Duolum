The Garden Wall

12 min read

The wall had been there longer than the house. Mara could tell by the way the moss had settled into it, filling every crack the way water fills a footprint, and by the stones themselves, which were the color of old bread.

She had come out at dawn because the kitchen felt wrong. Nothing had been moved and nothing was missing, but the whole room held its breath, the way a person does when they are pretending not to listen.

What the gardener left

Her grandfather had kept notebooks, dozens of them, stacked in the pantry behind the flour. Most were seed counts and weather. One page, folded into the back of the oldest, was different. It began with a line he had copied out in his small, upright hand:

A garden is a promise you keep to a stranger. Whoever comes after you will inherit every decision you made on your knees.

From Edmund Vale’s notebook, 1974

Mara read it twice. Below it, in the same careful hand, was something that was not a quotation at all.

third stone from the gate
seven steps toward the roses
dig where the light stops

She laughed, once, out loud, and the sound was strange in the empty garden. It was the kind of note a child writes when they want a treasure hunt. It was also, she realized, written in a hand that had never once been playful.

So she did what anyone would do. She made a list in the back of her own notebook, and then she went to find a spade.

  1. Find the third stone from the gate.
  2. Walk seven steps toward the roses.
  3. Dig where the light stops.

The third stone was easy. It was the only one with a small scar across its face, as if someone had once tried to split it and changed their mind. Seven steps took her into the old rose bed, where the bushes had grown so wild they leaned on each other for support.

She knelt. The earth was cold and smelled of iron. Within a few minutes the spade struck something that was not stone, and she set it aside and used her hands.

  • A trowel, rusted almost to the color of the soil.
  • A key wrapped in oilcloth, still faintly greasy.
  • A photograph of a woman she did not recognize, standing exactly where Mara now knelt.

On the back of the photograph there were three short lines, dated by someone who had clearly meant them to be found.

Date Entry Weather
4 May Planted the roses by the wall. Clear
19 May She has not come back. Rain
2 June Buried what she left. Told no one. Fog

By the time the sun cleared the roofline, Mara was sitting on the wall with the key in her lap and the photograph face down beside her, as though it might be embarrassed. Somewhere in the house a door opened and closed, softly, the way her grandfather used to close doors when he did not want to be noticed.

She had locked it behind her. She was almost sure of that.
